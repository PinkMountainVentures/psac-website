01 · Guide Structure

The guide is a
narrative arc.

Nine sections. Each one earns the next. The reader should feel the day moving forward before they've laced their boots. Three content types drive the system: fully dynamic content generated fresh from form inputs, library content selected and lightly personalized, and assembled content structured from inputs with fixed scaffolding.

DynamicGenerated fresh from form inputs. Hardest to automate. Highest impact.
LibrarySelected from pre-written library. Lightly personalized. Scalable.
AssembledFixed structure, dynamic detail slots from form inputs.
01
Cover
Assembled
Their name. The adventure name. The date. A single hero image of their specific terrain. Nothing else. Announces: this was made for you. Silent — the image does the work.
Dynamic elements: Name, adventure name, date, terrain image pulled from library by route selection.
02
The Opening — Your Day
Dynamic
Three to four sentences written directly to them. References something specific from their intake — why they're doing this, what they're after. The knowledgeable friend sitting down the night before. This section determines whether everything that follows lands. If it reads like a template, the experience collapses here.
Feeds from: Q1 (why they're going), Q2 (who's coming), Q3 (date/time), Q8 (what draws them). Entirely personalized — no library content here.
03
The Terrain
Library
One rich paragraph. A portrait of the place — what it feels like to be there at their specific time of day, what it looks like, what it smells like, what it asks. Present tense. Sensory without being purple. Ends at the trailhead threshold, handing directly to the route section. Pre-written and maintained as a library — one portrait per trail per time-of-day window. Written once, used indefinitely.
Feeds from: Route selection (Q5, Q9), time of day (Q3). Seasonal variant triggered by date input.
04
Your Route
Library
Waypoint-by-waypoint narrative. Start point, key waypoints, significant junctions, summit or destination. Each waypoint gets one to three sentences — what happens there, what to notice, what to do. Opinionated and specific. Includes the stop-and-look moment. May include a simple map embed or elevation profile. Route descriptions are pre-written per trail — selected by form, not generated fresh.
Feeds from: Q4 (experience), Q5 (activity), Q6 (duration), Q7 (physical considerations), Q8 (interests), Q9 (specific requests).
05
The Moments
Library
Two to three named experiences within the route worth slowing down for. Each gets a short title and two to three sentences. Set apart visually — pull-quoted or highlighted. Not "viewpoint at mile 3.2" but named, described, anticipated. Each route has three to four pre-written moments. Q8 multi-select surfaces the right two to three for this specific reader.
Feeds from: Q8 (what draws them — views, solitude, geology, water, wildlife, photography, etc).
06
Prepare Well
Assembled
Conversational paragraphs, not a checklist. Three short paragraphs — what to bring, when to go, what to expect from conditions. References what they said they're bringing and flags gaps. Adjusts for their specific date, start time, and any physical considerations. Core content is route and season-specific. Personal detail slots in from form inputs. Written as advice from someone who's done it — never as a liability document.
Feeds from: Q3 (date/time), Q7 (physical considerations), Q10 (gear checklist), Q11 (gear rental).
07
If the Day Changes
Library
Three to four short scenarios specific to this route — the things that actually go wrong on this terrain. Each has a trigger and a clear response. Positioned after Prepare Well, before After the Trail. Not labeled "Emergency Procedures" — titled something that fits the voice. Written calm and prepared, never alarming. Communicates that the whole day has been thought through, including the parts the reader hopes never to need. Personalizes based on whether they have a satellite communicator.
Feeds from: Q10 (satellite communicator flag), route-specific scenario library. Title options: "If the Day Changes" / "When the Mountain Decides."
08
After the Trail — Your Recovery
Assembled
The Peaks to Pools handoff. Written as continuation of the same day — not a separate section. The day isn't over yet. Two to three recovery recommendations with brief descriptions. Specific to what they told us recovery looks like — pool, meal, spa, back on the road, surprise me. Taste level signals tone and specificity. As hospitality partnerships formalize, pre-written recommendation descriptions slot in from a recovery library.
Feeds from: Q12 (how they want to feel), Q13 (recovery type), Q14 (taste level — simple / comfortable / elevated / surprise me).
09
A Note Before You Go
Dynamic
Three to five sentences. The warmest moment in the document. Pulls the emotional thread from the opening one final time — references what they're going out for, honors it, sends them off ready. Signed simply. Not a legal disclaimer. Not "have fun." Something that recognizes what they're about to do. The guide's voice, one last time.
Feeds from: Q1 (why they're going), Q12 (how they want to feel). Bookends the opening. Signed: PSAC, or guide author name when named guides are introduced.
02 · Intake Form

Fifteen questions.
Seven minutes.

The form is the beginning of the experience. The questions themselves — how they're worded, what they offer, the sequence — should feel like talking to someone who already understands your world. A progress indicator shows five named sections at the top. The section names reinforce the brand register with every glance.

Progress indicator sections:
Your Adventure · How You Move · What You're After · Your Kit · After the Trail
1
Your Adventure
Q1
What's bringing you out?
Fill-in-the-blank — pick a starter, complete it
Most important question on the form. Feeds the guide opening and closing note. The starters signal the emotional register PSAC operates in — being seen starts here.
"I need a day that's just mine —" "We're celebrating —" "I've been wanting to do this and I finally —" "I want to push myself —" "I'm showing someone I love this place —" "I just need to be outside and —"
Feeds: Guide opening (The Opening — Your Day), closing note (A Note Before You Go), closing screen dynamic paragraph.
Q2

Select + conditional follow-up
Just me My partner A small group of friends Family including kids

Q3

Date picker + time preference select
Early start (before 7am) Morning (7–9am) Mid-morning (after 9am) Flexible

04 · Terrain Portrait Format

The section that
transports them.

The terrain portrait is the only section in the guide that does nothing practical. It transports. Written in present tense. Sensory without being purple. Three paragraphs, a closing handoff line, and a time-of-day variant for each trail. Pre-written and maintained as a library — written once, used indefinitely. The competitive asset that cannot be quickly replicated.

Portrait Structure

200–250 words · Present tense · Two time-of-day variants per trail
Paragraph One
Arrival
What hits them first when they arrive. Not what they see — what they feel. The quality of the air, the sound, the scale, the specific thing that tells them they're in the right place. Grounds them physically in the place. One strong establishing image, then move. Written specifically to their time of day — the desert at 6am and 9am are genuinely different places.
Paragraph Two
Character
The deeper character of the place. Its history, its weight, what makes it distinct from every other trail in the desert. This is where the portrait earns its premium — not describing terrain but telling the truth about the place. The writing that makes someone feel they already know it before they've been. Avoids the generic, the guidebook, the obvious. Goes somewhere the AllTrails review never does.
Paragraph Three
What It Asks
What the place asks of the person who walks into it. Not the physical ask — the psychological or emotional one. Does it ask for patience? Attention? Surrender? A willingness to be uncomfortable before it pays off? Shifts the reader from observer to participant. They're not going to look at a canyon. They're going to be in a conversation with it.
Closing Line
Handoff
One sentence. Present tense. Hands them to the route section — the portrait ends at the threshold, the route begins on the other side. Should feel like a door opening. Plain and true rather than reaching for profundity. Example: "Step in. The canyon takes it from here." The route section picks up immediately after this line.
Voice filters — apply to every portrait
Cut Sentences that explain what the reader should feel rather than making them feel it. Adjective stacking — two adjectives where one is stronger. Scene-setting that runs past the establishing shot. Any sentence that reaches for profundity rather than saying the true thing plainly.
Keep Specific sensory detail over general description. Plain sentences that say the true thing. Present tense throughout. The line that surprises — the detail nobody else mentions. Anything that makes the reader feel they've already arrived.
05 · Writing Workflow

Brain dump.
Compress. Truth check.

A repeatable three-step process for every portrait. The brain dump is where verbosity is an asset — write fast, don't edit, let it run. The compression pass filters it down to the portrait format. The truth check is the author's alone — nobody else can confirm whether the compressed version still holds the essential truth of the place.

1
Brain Dump
Write without constraints. Answer the five questions below in any order, without editing. The goal is to get actual knowledge and feeling about the place onto the page — not to write the portrait yet. Verbosity is an asset here. Let it run.
Target time: 15–20 minutes. Medium: Notes app, doc, voice memo transcribed — anything that gets it out fast.
2
Compression Pass
Bring the brain dump here. Apply the voice filters — cut over-explanation, adjective stacking, reaching for profundity. Shape into the three-paragraph structure with closing handoff line. Target 200–250 words. Check against time-of-day variant if writing multiple versions.
Target time: 20–25 minutes with editorial support. After two or three portraits the compression instinct develops and passes get faster.
3
Truth Check
Read the compressed portrait against actual memory of the place. Does it feel true? Does it capture the essential thing — the thing that makes this trail worth recommending? If something essential got cut in compression, it goes back in and something else comes out. This step belongs to the author alone.
Target time: 5–10 minutes. The final authority is always: does this feel like the place?
The Brain Dump Prompt
Five questions for every portrait. Answer freely, in any order, without editing. Write fast. Tell the truth about the place.
1
The Arrival
What hits you first when you get to this trailhead? Not what you see — what you feel. The quality of the air, the sound, the scale of what's in front of you, the specific thing that tells you you're in the right place. What's the first thirty seconds like before you've taken a single step on trail?
2
What Most People Miss
You've been here more than once. You know something about this trail that someone reading AllTrails wouldn't know. A specific moment, a section, a view, a detail, a time of day, a direction of travel. What's the thing that makes your version of this trail different from the generic version?
3
What It Asks of You
Every trail has a character — something it demands or invites from the person walking it. Not the physical stats. The psychological or emotional ask. Does it ask for patience? Attention? Surrender? A willingness to be uncomfortable before it pays off? What does this trail want from you?
4
The Moment
There's at least one moment on this trail that makes everything worth it. Name it specifically. Where are you on the trail, what are you looking at or feeling, what does it do to you. If you were sending a friend and could only tell them one thing to watch for — what would it be?
5
The Return
How do you feel coming off this trail? Not physically — emotionally. What does the trail leave you with? What's different about you at the trailhead on the way out versus the way in? This is the feeling the portrait is ultimately promising the reader.
Lykken System Only — Question 6
Why this door in? This is one of several ways onto this trail system. What does this specific entry point give someone that the others don't? What kind of person or day does this trailhead suit best? Why would you send someone here instead of the other entries?
06 · Difficulty Tiers

Four tiers.
Every customer served.

Volume comes from moderate trails. Credibility comes from expert routes. The family tier is the most underserved segment in the Palm Springs outdoor market — and the one with the highest lifetime value. Library mix target: 40% accessible/family, 30% moderate, 20% expert, 10% extended/multi-element.

Family with Young Children
Trails validated with young children — written for the parent, not the child. Honest about pace, shade, bailout points, what captures a toddler's attention, actual time at a child's pace. Portrait format adds a sixth question: what does a young child encounter here that's genuinely remarkable? The parent who has a transcendent experience in Andreas Canyon with their three-year-old becomes a customer for life.

08 · Seasonal Framework

Four operating
windows.

The desert is not the same place in November and June. Seasonal awareness isn't a safety disclaimer — it's expert knowledge expressed as service. The guide's job is to match the right trail to the right customer on the right day, and that requires honest seasonal routing. In summer, the library shrinks. The guide says so directly, and redirects with confidence.

Window One · Peak
November — February
The sweet spot. Comfortable temperatures across most of the day. Full library available. Crowds higher but conditions forgiving. This is when you can send almost anyone almost anywhere. Portrait library at its fullest range.
Full library accessible · All tiers available · Time of day flexible
Window Two · Shoulder Spring
March — April
Transitional and often spectacular. Wildflower season in some areas. Mornings still cool but afternoons can surprise. Start time begins to matter more. Bighorn sheep lambing season — worth noting for family portraits. Canyon and shaded routes remain fully viable.
Most library accessible · Morning start recommended · Expert routes viable early
Window Three · Shoulder Fall
May · October
Early starts become non-negotiable on exposed trails. Some routes close as responsible recommendations for moderate hikers. Expert routes accessible for people who know heat management and start before dawn. The desert starts showing its teeth — which is part of the experience for the right customer.
Reduced library · Early start non-negotiable · Expert routes: dawn only
Window Four · Summer
June — September
Genuinely dangerous for most of the library. The guide does not send people into harm's way — it redirects with confidence. Viable options: shaded canyons, higher elevation routes, Whitewater Preserve (year-round river), early morning expert routes for experienced athletes who know desert heat management deeply.
Minimal library · Canyon and riparian only · Whitewater viable · Redirect with confidence
Seasonal routing principle
When the date + trail combination creates a safety concern, the guide doesn't add a warning label — it reroutes. The knowledgeable friend says: "I hear what you want, and here's what actually serves you on this date." That redirection, delivered with confidence rather than apology, is itself a demonstration of the premium service. A pamphlet warns. A guide redirects.

The guide is not written from scratch for every customer. It's a system that generates the personal parts, selects the right library content, and assembles the structured parts from form inputs. The result feels entirely personal. The effort is sustainable at five hours per week.

What gets built
first.

Sequenced by impact and dependency. Portrait writing begins with trails only PSAC can write — the ones where the knowledge is the entire asset. The form is built in parallel with the first portrait batch. The guide template is built once the first five portraits exist.

Write Priority Portrait Batch One — Five Trails
Maynard Mine · Cathedral Canyon — Art Smith — Desert Riders Oasis · South Lykken South Trailhead · Henderson — Shannon — Berns — Garstin · Bogert — Wildhorse — Clara Burgess to Murray Peak. Use brain dump prompt → compression pass workflow. Two time-of-day variants each.
Write Priority Portrait Batch Two — Family Trails
Murray Canyon · Victor — Palm Canyon · Andreas Canyon · Tahquitz Canyon · North Lykken Ramon · Big Morongo Canyon · Painted Canyon Ladder · Whitewater Preserve. Family portrait format with Q6 child variant. The most underserved segment in the market.
Build JotForm Consultation Intake — 15 Questions, 5 Sections
Card format, progress indicator with section names, fill-in-the-blank mechanics for Q1 and Q12, conditional logic for Q2 group/family follow-up. Closing screen with dynamic paragraph from Q1 + Q12 inputs and one-tap rating.
Build Guide Template — Document Structure and Visual System
Nine-section layout in PSAC brand. Cover, Opening, Terrain, Route, Moments, Prepare Well, If the Day Changes, After the Trail, Closing Note. Slots for library content and dynamic content clearly defined. Format that feels premium on screen and in print.
Define Routing Logic — Form Inputs to Guide Sections
Map every form input to its guide output. Seasonal check logic. Difficulty routing from Q4 experience signal. Portrait variant selection from Q3 time of day. Q8 to Moments selection. Q12–14 to After the Trail tone and recommendations.
Build Post-Guide Feedback — 24–48hr Follow-up Email
Single question: "Did we get it right?" Five-star rating plus optional text. Sent after estimated adventure date. Measures whether the promise landed in the real world — different signal than the form closing screen rating, which measures intake experience only.
Tag Every Trail in the Library
Apply all eight tag dimensions to every trail in the current library. Difficulty · Season (per month) · Time of day · Activity · Shade profile · Water · Duration at hiker's pace · Wildlife considerations. Tagging system is what enables correct routing at scale.
